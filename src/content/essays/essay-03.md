# Why We Still Read Slowly

The internet has become remarkably good at helping us move.

We scroll, swipe, tap, search, skip, refresh, and repeat. Within seconds we can read headlines from five countries, compare cameras, book a flight, and forget why we opened the browser in the first place.

Moving quickly has become effortless.

Paying attention has not.

Somewhere along the way, reading became something to optimize. Articles were shortened. Paragraphs became bullet points. Headlines competed for clicks instead of clarity. Entire industries emerged around measuring how long someone stayed on a page before leaving it again.

None of these changes happened with bad intentions. They simply reflected the incentives of the modern web.

Speed became success.

## Reading is different

Reading has never been about reaching the end as quickly as possible.

No one rushes through a favorite novel simply to finish it. We don't admire a photograph by glancing at it for half a second before moving on to the next one.

The value isn't found at the end.

It's found in the time spent paying attention.

Essays work the same way.

A good essay doesn't deliver information as efficiently as possible. It slowly builds an idea, introducing one thought after another until something larger begins to emerge.

That process cannot be rushed.

## The quiet value of staying

When we stay with a piece of writing, something interesting happens.

Our minds begin making connections that weren't explicitly written.

A sentence reminds us of a conversation.

A paragraph recalls a place we visited years ago.

An argument changes our opinion—not instantly, but gradually, almost without noticing.

None of this appears in analytics.

There is no metric for the moment an idea quietly changes someone's perspective.

Yet those moments are often the reason we write at all.

## Designing for attention

This is one of the ideas behind Still.

The theme doesn't exist to impress visitors during their first few seconds.

It exists to support the next ten minutes.

That influences nearly every design decision.

Typography is chosen for comfort rather than novelty.

Margins create room for thought.

Animations are restrained.

Navigation avoids unnecessary complexity.

Even silence becomes part of the interface.

Design can either compete with writing or support it.

Still tries to choose the latter.

## There is no finish line

The strange thing about reading is that it rarely ends when we close the page.

Good writing lingers.

An observation returns while washing dishes.

A question resurfaces during a walk.

A single sentence unexpectedly appears weeks later during another conversation.

Books have always done this.

Essays can too.

Their influence often begins after we've stopped reading.

## The slow web

There is growing interest in what some people call the "slow web."

It's less a movement than a shared instinct.

Instead of chasing constant updates, it values permanence.

Instead of endless feeds, it favors archives.

Instead of publishing dozens of times each day, it celebrates publishing something worth returning to years later.

A personal website is uniquely suited to this way of thinking.

It doesn't need to maximize engagement.

It doesn't need to satisfy an algorithm.

It simply needs to remain available whenever someone arrives.

## Writing for one reader

One consequence of modern publishing is the temptation to imagine enormous audiences.

Thousands of readers.

Millions of impressions.

Charts that rise every month.

In reality, most memorable writing happens because one person had something worth saying.

Imagine a single thoughtful reader.

Write for them.

Not because your audience will always be small, but because clarity scales better than performance.

A conversation always begins with two people.

## Leaving room

One of the hardest parts of writing is knowing what to leave unsaid.

The same is true for design.

Not every page needs a sidebar.

Not every sentence needs emphasis.

Not every thought needs immediate resolution.

Space allows readers to participate.

When every inch of a page demands attention, nothing truly receives it.

Whitespace isn't empty.

It's an invitation.

## Returning

The internet encourages novelty.

There is always another article.

Another newsletter.

Another opinion.

Reading slowly offers something different.

It invites us to return.

To reread.

To notice details we missed the first time.

Some essays become companions rather than destinations.

They grow richer because we have changed since the last visit.

Perhaps that's one of the quiet joys of keeping a personal website.

Not every piece needs to go viral.

Some only need to be waiting patiently for the reader who finds them exactly when they need them.

In a web that increasingly rewards speed, choosing to write—and to read—slowly remains a remarkably hopeful act.
