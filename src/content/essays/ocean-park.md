It wasn't unusual for me to come across an area in the process of renovation, and to surprise myself not just by the extent of the demolished areas, but by the half-torn-down buildings –– still functional –– with their innards on display.

![Man overlooking construction site](/images/oceanPark-02.webp "Man overlooking construction site")

The sight of high rises under construction was an ever-present image in the background of the city. 

![Housing development in the outskirts of Hanoi](/images/oceanPark-03.webp "Housing development in the outskirts of Hanoi")

Not much has changed since my initial days in Vietnam. If anything, the rate of development has increased to accommodate the country's massive economical and urbanization growth.

But as the streets of Hanoi become saturated, developers have come up with new strategies for expansion.

## Corporate Cities

![View of Mega Grand World Hanoi](/images/oceanPark-04.webp "View of Mega Grand World Hanoi")

Walking along the artificial river inside Mega Grand World Hanoi on a weekday, lines of empty pastel-colored buildings surrounded us. 

Motorized venetian gondolas took tourists on a loop of the river, marble statues greeted at us, pop music blasted at full volume across the area's speakers, private security drove around in electric motorbikes.

My main thought as I walked through these streets was:

> All of this development –– dozens of apartment buildings across three sites, minimarkets, malls, electric cars, hospitals, schools & universities, roads and other infrastructures –– belongs to a single company, VinGroup, a massive Vietnamese conglomerate.

Marketed to investors and consumers as a *world-class mega city*, a *resort paradise*. Smart, sustainable, and of course, great ROI.

To be fair, on paper, it's an idyllic place to live. Perfectly manicured and conveniently featured for most of our human needs. 

The question then is, what's wrong about this?

## How cities are born

![Scene in the alleys of Âu Cơ](/images/oceanPark-08.webp "Scene in the alleys of Âu Cơ")

Though there have been examples of well-designed cities across history, most cities were not consciously "designed" at all. They emerged through long negotiations between geography, economy, culture, and power.

Comparatively, places such as Ocean Park follow a different logic altogether. 

They are products. 

Designed to attract capital first, and life –– so long as it supports the investment narrative –– second.

It's not hard to imagine a future where this is the norm. Where decisions are made in advance by a paternalistic company on a quest for profit. Where most, if not all, verticals of a society are owned by a corporate entity and turned into a closed business loop. Where residency is endowed by subscription, and can be easily revoked if the terms and conditions are not met.

## The *enshittification* of housing

First coined by Cory Doctorow in 2022, [enshittification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enshittification) is the process through which products and services decline in quality over time.

Though initially expressed in terms of modern digital platforms, this concept can also be applied to other industries, such as housing. In fact there's historical precedence of this already happening in the form of [company towns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pullman_Company).

Once the incentives for building livable environments are replaced by the incentives for rent extraction and capital preservation, the path forward is simple: reduce costs, tighten control, and shift risk onto residents.
