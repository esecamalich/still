With the prospect of my time in Bali coming to an end after 6 years, the last few weeks have been marked by an increased effort in photographing what I'm hoping to be the end of [https://strangevisitations.com/](Strange Visitations), my long term photographic project shot in the island.

For this last bit of the process, I'm focusing on an ever-reducing portion of the island: Ubud's Art Market.

After having spent years documenting far and wide across Bali, trying to expand my understanding of it and make peace with its nuances and my position inside of it all, I finally feel ready to make my closing statement.

Why Ubud's Market? Because in my very few years here, I've seen the flow of history pass through it. Flattened by the pandemic period –– when long-term immigrants would tell me how Bali felt like having gone back to the pre-mass-tourism era –– demolished, reconstructed and burnt, all the way to its first LED marketing screen, now running at full speed around the tourist seasons.

Sharing a jagged corner with Ubud's Palace, government offices and temples, this intersection is the heart of the tourist industry in the city. From here, one can walk in all four directions through roads lined with restaurants, souvenir shops, massage parlors, tour operators, boutiques, homestays, and all the necessary amenities for a pleasant holiday experience.

At the top of the market's corner, there is an esplanade with a statue of Tjokorda Gde Agung Sukawati, king of Ubud, famous for having promoted tourism and arts along the likes of Walter Spies and Rudolf Bonnet. Its cement base almost always full with *canang sari*. It is in this same corner where royal cremations begin their arduous procession towards the Pura Dalem Puri, the temple where these ceremonies are held.

What once was a decaying building is now a lively spot where people sit and watch others pass, gelato in hand. Kids sneakily pass behind the market guards, hoping to score a quick sale before they get kicked out of the premises.

"100k. 80k. 50k. Ok, 20," they bargain. "Ok, an ice cream," they settle for.

In the Starbucks nearby, the first in the city but not the last, a construction is happening outside its walls, just barely out of sight from the windows from where their clients with their grande frappuccinos look out into the city. Inside the ditch below, two men from Java take a break watching TikTok on their phones –– they've been here only a month, no shoes, no safety equipment. Only grit and their will to send money back home.

Having said all this, what is my closing statement?

For many, this is Bali's experience.
